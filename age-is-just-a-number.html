HORROR & SPECULATIVE FICTION CONTACT

AGE IS JUST NUMBER

I woke in the dark to the muffled ring of my phone alarm, chiming from somewhere beyond the reach of my hand. I opened my eyes cautiously, straining for any sight or sound, anything that might confirm the fear rising in my chest. Was it still the same day as before? I couldn’t tell anymore. The days had collapsed into one another, an unbroken stretch of tedium and meaninglessness, each indistinguishable from the last.

I forced myself upright and groped across the bed, fingers skimming rumpled sheets in search of the phone. My foot caught in the fabric and I nearly pitched over the edge. The sudden lurch of gravity snapped me briefly awake. I froze there, half-sitting, half-falling, and a tired thought drifted through me: maybe I should just stay in bed. Do nothing. Leave the world undisturbed. No motion, no decisions, nothing that might provoke a chain of events, spreading outward like ripples across still water. Perhaps it would be better, I told myself, to pretend I didn’t exist at all.

But the heavy, relentless pounding in my chest and the cold that crept down my spine betrayed me. They were proof, cruel and undeniable, that I did exist, bound to this moment, this body, this particular thread in the universe’s timeline.

I was born on January 13th, 1989, at six minutes past seven in the evening. Why that day? Why that precise hour of the night? I often find myself circling these questions as if they might eventually yield something tangible. If I had been born earlier or later by even a fraction of time, what might have shifted? Would my parents have been happier? Would their separation, so inevitable in retrospect, have been delayed or averted altogether? Would I have grown into someone else entirely, someone steadier, someone less fragile? Of course, no one can answer such absurd and unanswerable questions, yet I have been dwelling on them for days now. Whenever the darkness returns, as it always does, I begin to interrogate my own existence, as though searching for a hairline crack in the grand architecture of the universe where everything went irreversibly wrong.

Before it is too late, I want to offer an apology to anyone I may have hurt along the way. If you ever reached out to me with kindness and I mistook it for intrusion, if I recoiled when all you intended was warmth, then I am truly sorry. My personality feels attenuated, stretched thin across a narrow psychological bandwidth, and I have never learned how to occupy the same emotional space as another person without panic or withdrawal. I do not know how to conduct myself in ordinary human exchanges. Even the simplest interaction feels like foreign terrain. I often think of myself as a lost cause. The only light I can still discern comes from distant, fading memories of a life I once had, or perhaps imagined having, many years ago. Those fragments are all I cling to now. One misstep, one moment of carelessness, and I feel myself slipping, tumbling back into the void I have spent so long trying to avoid.

A few months ago, on my thirty second birthday, I made one of the most reckless and costly decisions of my life. It was not driven by logic or desire, but by loneliness and quiet desperation. Growing up in one of Russia’s northernmost and coldest towns, with a mother who was herself struggling with mental illness, had left its mark on me long before I understood what was happening. I had been battling depression for years, but it deepened into something heavier after my mother passed away two years ago. Her death hollowed out what little stability I had managed to build. I found myself confronting thoughts I had once sworn I would never entertain. With no remaining family and no close friends to speak of, I learned to swallow disappointment in solitude, rehearsing my failures in silence. I worked from home, grateful for the isolation it afforded and relieved not to face people or their expectations. It was Filip, a Polish friend I occasionally connected with over our shared love of photography, who gently suggested one day that I see a psychiatrist. At the time, it felt less like advice and more like a last, uncertain hand reaching out in the dark.

“What? That’s the kind of thing rich people do. I’m not rich,” I said, barely looking up as I adjusted the contrast on a photo glowing on my laptop screen.

“You’re not married. No kids. You live alone,” Filip replied evenly. “What have you got to lose?”

It stung more than I expected. Still, he was not wrong. Filip had always been like that. No nonsense, no softening the truth to spare feelings. He never dressed things up nicely, but he was also the first to reach out when I fell apart, the first to haul me back onto my feet and brush the dust from my shoulders. That was why our friendship endured when so many others quietly faded away.

“So? If I lost my mind tomorrow, or next month, nobody would really cry for me,” I said with a shrug. “So who would care, anyway?”

He frowned and stepped closer. “I would care. I would cry if you died,” he said, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. “We’re friends, yeah?”

He gave my shoulder a firm squeeze and set a cup of hot coffee down beside my laptop. The steam curled upward between us, filling the room with warmth.

That small, almost careless act of kindness was what finally broke my resistance. It was the reason I gave in and, reluctantly and with no small amount of resentment, booked an appointment with one of the most prominent psychologists in town.

She was a kind, motherly woman in her late fifties, with a calm voice that never rushed me. At the start of every session, she would ask whether I felt alright that day and if I wanted to continue, as though giving me permission to back out at any moment. In the beginning, I found it nearly impossible to open up to her. She was a stranger, after all, and I had spent most of my life learning how to keep people at a safe distance. But her patience never wavered. Session by session, her gentleness wore me down, and slowly, against my instincts, I began to trust her.

She told me I showed signs of severe social inhibition and that the only way forward was to learn how to open up, little by little, starting with her and with Filip. She urged me to stop rehearsing every possible outcome in my head, to stop exhausting myself with endless what-ifs.

“Why don’t you go out and strike up conversations with people on the street?” she suggested one afternoon. “Ask if you can take their picture for free. People love free things. And having their photo taken by a good photographer like you? Most won’t pass that up.”

“But there’ll be a lot of people,” I said quietly.

“Yes,” she replied, smiling. “That’s the point. Face what scares you. Why don’t you go out and take pictures? As many as you want. Then choose the ones that make you feel something good. The ones that make you smile, even just a little. Print them. Frame them. Put them on your wall.”

She told me I had been living alone for far too long, and that solitude, in my case, was doing more harm than good. She suggested I rent out my house and move in with a friend. I found the idea ridiculous. The house was the only truly valuable thing I had ever owned, and it carried too many memories of my childhood and of my mother. Letting go of it felt unthinkable. Besides, I am a compulsive hoarder, and I doubted anyone would have the patience to tolerate my habits. Still, I knew she was right. Everything she had asked me to do up to that point had genuinely improved my self-esteem, even when I resisted it at first. So, instead of arguing with her advice, I decided to do something spontaneous, just as she had suggested earlier.

I began cautiously, taking candid photographs of passersby from the safety of my front window. Strangers walking their dogs, couples passing without noticing me, people moving through their lives while I observed from a distance. After that, I gathered enough courage to visit a nearby beach and photograph people there. Strangers again, but closer this time. One step at a time, she had said. She also encouraged me to reward myself with something pleasant whenever I managed to complete a task or overcome a fear, no matter how small it seemed. And so I did. I faced my fears, slowly and deliberately.

For years, Filip had been asking me to come along whenever he went out with his friends. And for years, I had always said no. I knew he had plenty of other people to drink with. People who were easier company, who didn’t stare too long into nothing, who didn’t carry themselves like a shell-shocked World War II veteran who had wandered into the wrong decade.

That afternoon, though, something in me shifted. Maybe I was tired of declining. Maybe I was tired of myself. In a half-hearted attempt to be more spontaneous and less depressed, I picked up my phone and texted him. I asked if he wanted to grab a drink later. Or, if he already had plans, whether I could tag along.

He didn’t reply. Instead, my phone rang almost immediately.

“Are you serious?” he asked, before I could even say hello.

“Yes,” I said. Then, when he paused, I said it again. “Yeah. I’m serious.”

He laughed, surprised, and I could hear the excitement creeping into his voice. He told me he’d love to have me there, that his friends would be happy to meet me. His close friends, his good friends, as he kept emphasizing, as if reassuring both of us at once. He said he’d pick me up around nine.

On the drive downtown, heading toward his friend’s place where we’d be hanging out well past midnight, he wouldn’t stop talking about how much I was going to love it. How his friends were solid guys. Good people. The kind you could trust.

And then it hit me: he had already briefed them. Given them the quiet rundown. Be gentle. Don’t push. Watch what you say.

I hated that.

I knew I was fragile, thin ice stretched over a frozen lake in the dead of winter, but I still hated the idea that people had to adjust themselves around me, rearrange their personalities, soften their edges just to keep me from cracking. It made me feel weak. Exposed. Useless.

Still, I didn’t say anything. Filip meant well. He always did. He probably knew me better than anyone else alive, except maybe myself. So I nodded along, forced a smile when he glanced over at me, and tried to look excited at the thought of spending hours with four other grown men, drinking until the night dissolved into something blurry and loud. Whether I was ready for it or not.

Luckily, it went better than I’d expected. His friends were exactly as he’d promised: warm, a bit too loud, but effortlessly friendly. They welcomed me with open arms, the kind of enthusiasm that made it feel as if we’d all known each other for years instead of minutes. One of them, Georgi, seemed to take a particular interest in me. He kept inching closer on the couch, refilling my glass before I’d even finished the last one, insisting I drink more. Maybe he thought the alcohol would loosen me up, dissolve whatever stiffness I’d brought into the room. I couldn’t really tell.

There was something about the way they treated me that felt slightly staged, though. Genuine, yes, but rehearsed. Like a kindness they’d practiced beforehand. I imagined Filip briefing them in advance, laying out strict instructions, possibly under the threat of medieval punishment. Be nice to him. Don’t scare him. Don’t raise your voice. Filip himself kept glancing my way whenever the jokes got louder or the laughter edged toward shouting, as if bracing for me to crack apart right there in the living room.

Later that morning, as he drove me home through empty streets washed pale by early daylight, he wouldn’t stop talking. He told me how proud he was of me, how well I’d done, how we should do this more often. I nodded along, though I was far too drunk to keep up with most of what he was saying. Georgi had seen to that. The idea of this becoming a regular thing didn’t exactly fill me with excitement. I wasn’t trying to reinvent myself overnight. I was just trying to pry myself open a little. One night at a time. It was the vodka talking probably anyway. Once it worked its way out of my system, I’d probably snap right back into my usual grim shape. And honestly, I was already bracing for that moment.

My progress with my therapist went better than I had expected, despite my lingering suspicion that therapy was, at least in part, pretentious. When I first started seeing her, we met two, sometimes three times a week. At the time, I needed the structure. The routine. Somewhere to unload my thoughts before they spiraled too far out of reach.

As weeks passed and things began to stabilize, I was the one who brought it up.

“Is it normal to cut back at some point?” I asked during one session. “Like… once a week?”

She considered it for a moment, then nodded.

“If you feel ready for that, yes. Progress also means learning to carry some of the weight on your own.”

She agreed to reduce our sessions, on the condition that I keep a weekly journal. Nothing elaborate. Just a record of how I was feeling, what I was struggling with, and what had helped. I hated the idea at first. It felt like homework. Still, I did it.

At one point, she floated a different kind of suggestion.

“What about traveling?” she asked casually, as if she were asking whether I had tried a new brand of tea. “A short trip across South America or Southeast Asia, where it’s much warmer.”

I blinked at her. “I’ve never been abroad,” I admitted. “And I’m pretty sure I can’t afford it.”

She smiled, unfazed.

“Some places are much less expensive than people think, once you’ve covered the flight. My husband and I went to Indonesia last year. Food, transportation, accommodation. Everything was so affordable we actually had more spending power there than we do here.”

“Wow,” I said. “That sounds… nice.”

“It was,” she replied. “And it was refreshing. You should experience that at least once.”

I shrugged. “I don’t know. I’m probably too old to be flying halfway across the world.”

She shook her head, still smiling, but there was something firmer beneath it this time.

“You need to stop treating your life like it’s an expired contract,” she said. “You are not past your due date. You can go anywhere and do whatever you want at any age. The calendar doesn’t get to decide your worth unless you let it.”

I looked down at my hands, unconvinced but listening.

“Age is just a number,” she continued, more gently now. “And numbers only have power if you give it to them.”

Looking back now, I can see that things had already begun to lighten at that point in my life. The weight wasn’t gone, not really, but it wasn’t crushing me the way it used to. I no longer spent every night alone with my darker thoughts, masturbating occasionally for the brief chemical relief it offered, then staring into the hours until morning. I still avoided people, still kept to myself. But the idea of going out, of being around strangers, no longer filled me with dread.

That was when the thought first took hold. Maybe I should leave. Just for a while. Go somewhere far from the cold, from the bleak landscape I’d grown used to. Somewhere warm. Somewhere bright. Somewhere that felt like the opposite of everything I’d been living inside.

Originally, I had planned to take a vacation to Southeast Asia for my birthday. Maybe a week in Thailand or Bali. But I changed my mind. The thought of crowded streets, unfamiliar places, languages and accents I didn’t understand or anything that might spiral out of control was enough to trigger a panic attack. My chest would tighten, my vision blur, my breathing turn shallow. In the end, I scrapped the plan entirely.

Still, a thought lingered at the back of my mind, quietly urging me to do something different. Something drastic, even. Maybe it would be the push I needed to open myself up a little more. Or maybe it would end in disaster. Either way, staying frozen in place, endlessly turning the same thoughts over in my head, wasn’t getting me anywhere. My slow, uneven journey toward something like healing had been going well enough. I couldn’t give up now. Not yet.

Then one morning, sitting in my usual corner of the bedroom, camera resting in my hands as I stared out the window, the answer presented itself. It felt sudden, almost embarrassing in its simplicity. A small, private gotcha moment. I felt a smile tug at the corner of my mouth and slowly spread as I watched the elderly woman across the street make her daily trip to the grocery store, her squat, brown Pekingese waddling dutifully at her side.

That was it. That was exactly what I needed. A companion. Not another person, but something living, something uncomplicated. A puppy. That was the radical thing I’d been circling without realizing it. A pet. I’d never had one before. My mother’s chunky goldfish in our old apartment didn’t count.

The next morning, I woke earlier than usual, already buzzing with a nervous kind of excitement. I took a long, hot shower, my mind racing ahead of me, cycling through decisions I hadn’t yet earned. What kind of dog? What breed? How I’d have to rearrange my apartment to make it pet friendly. What I would name it. By the time I stepped out, I felt strangely certain, as if the decision had already been made without my consent.

I pulled on my coat and all but leapt out the door, starting the short walk through the neighborhood. There was an animal shelter somewhere nearby that took in strays and unwanted pets from all over the city. Filip had been there once, years ago, to get his daughter a hamster for her seventh birthday. He’d told me the place was small, crowded, and louder than you’d expect.

On the way there, my phone suddenly buzzed with an email notification while I was following Google Maps. I tapped it absentmindedly. It was a forwarded message from an unknown sender, containing a digital pamphlet from a company I had never heard of called Depresso Inc. The tagline read, Feeling depressed? Suicidal? Well it’s time to stop. Be positive.

I scrolled further and realized it was an advertisement for a newly released smart Android robot called Depresso Plus. According to the description, it was designed to assist people with mental illnesses by helping them manage anxiety and suicidal thoughts. There were photos of a small, slender humanoid robot available in three colors, dark cyan, maroon, and teal. Its functions were similar to any virtual assistant, with a crucial difference. It was fully autonomous, and its primary purpose was mental health support. It could organize schedules, remind you to take medication, and recite daily motivational quotes meant to lift your spirits.

It also came equipped with a small cylindrical glass compartment at the back, designed to release soothing fragrances to aid relaxation and sleep. A flashlight-like protrusion on its head emitted a soft blue glow whenever you entered the room. Its screen displayed cheerful smiling faces at all times, supposedly to stimulate positive thinking. On top of that, it offered easy access to interactive mental health websites without the exhausting and endless registration processes that usually discouraged people. It was marketed specifically toward individuals who were too anxious or ashamed to seek help from others, fearful of judgment and stigma.

For obvious reasons, it caught my attention. It was expensive, but not beyond my means. I slowed to a stop on the sidewalk, the shelter only a few blocks away, as skepticism and a fragile kind of hope began to wrestle in my head. Questions crowded in all at once. Could this be what I needed right now? Something precise. Controlled. Designed for people like me. I’d never owned anything particularly high tech beyond my cameras, but the idea lodged itself stubbornly in place.

Then there was the dog. I was already halfway there, both literally and mentally. A living thing was a serious commitment. Feeding. Walking. Cleaning. Worrying. Being needed every hour of every day. Was I actually ready for that? Or was I only romanticizing the idea because it felt warm and simple from a distance?

A small robot with artificial intelligence, on the other hand, didn’t need food or walks. It wouldn’t shed or get sick or stare at me with disappointed eyes if I stayed in bed too long. It would be like a smartphone that could move, listen, and respond. And according to the ad, it was specifically designed to help people with mental illnesses. That alone made my chest tighten. Maybe this was the safer option. Still a companion. Still something to come home to. Just without the mess, the noise, or the risk of failing another living being.

I stood there longer than I meant to, the cold creeping through my coat, the decision pressing heavier by the second. Before I could second-guess myself, I clicked the ad, turned around, and walked back the way I’d come, already imagining what my therapist would make of it. She wouldn’t be angry. That almost made it worse. She’d probably sigh, tilt her head, and ask why I thought a machine could do what people couldn’t. I could already hear the disappointment I’d project onto her voice. Still, at least I’d have the robot. Right?

I waited nearly a month for it to arrive. When I finally turned it on, the small teal robot, which I immediately nicknamed DEP, short for Depresso, not very creative, released a sweet, fruity scent that instantly transported me back to my childhood on the apple farm. From that moment on, DEP began helping me manage my chaotic schedule and broken sleep patterns.

Every morning, it woke me with a gentle, non-intrusive alarm, followed by an enthusiastic stream of phrases like, “Rise and shine, fighter,” “Remember, you are loved,” “Love for all, hatred for none,” and “Be yourself, change the world.” I was fairly certain it pulled most of them from the internet. From time to time throughout the day, it would remind me to hydrate for “better evening performance,” whatever that meant.

DEP also came with a huge catalogue of calming ambient sounds: beach waves, rainfall, tropical forests, and more. Once, out of curiosity, I tried one of its newer sound settings, a recording of whales singing. That was a mistake. I dreamed that night of sinking into dark water, surrounded by enormous shapes, their haunting calls echoing all around me.

I know it probably sounds ridiculous, but for someone who lived in constant fear of humiliation and rejection, it felt comforting to wake up to DEP’s clumsy motivational quotes instead of my own dark and punishing thoughts. As it turned out, the robot was every bit as intelligent as the advertisement promised.

“It observes and it learns,” the technician said when he delivered it to my house. He looked like a stereotypical tech nerd, thick glasses, wide grin, barely containing his excitement. “The more you interact with it, the smarter it gets. It remembers what you say.”

He went on to explain that DEP’s advanced voice and facial recognition systems allowed it to assess my emotional state in real time. If it detected signs of anxiety or an impending panic attack, it would automatically alert my designated contact by phone call, which, in my case, was Filip. As the system continued to learn, it would begin making unsolicited suggestions, offering help even when I had not asked for it. Before leaving, the technician recommended that I keep DEP’s humor setting below sixty percent for the first few weeks so that I could, as he put it, take it seriously. I ignored that advice entirely and set it to one hundred percent. I needed to laugh.

As I’d half expected, my therapist didn’t seem particularly convinced by the idea of an AI robot serving as a companion for someone like me. She hid it well, though. At least, she tried to. Her face remained neutral as I explained myself, but I could sense the hesitation beneath it.

“Actually,” she said after a moment, jotting something down on her clipboard, “I’ve been thinking about introducing you to another client of mine.”

“Really?” I said, genuinely surprised.

She nodded, then hesitated, shrugging slightly as if reconsidering the idea even as she voiced it.

“She’s dealing with similar issues,” she said, “but she’s been making good progress. It can help to know someone who understands what you’re going through. Think of it as peer support, if you will.”

She’s a she?

“That… well that sounds like a great idea,” I replied, blinking, a grin spreading across my face before I could stop it. Oh, great. She’s hooking crazy people up now too, I thought grimly. Was this some kind of side hustle? In this economy, I wouldn’t have been surprised.

“You know what? Maybe another time, okay?” she waved it off.

“No, seriously. That might actually be a good idea,” I said, scratching my chin. “When can I meet her?”

“First things first,” she said with a small, patient smile. “Let’s talk about this robot.”

I tensed a little, as if she’d just mentioned something embarrassing I’d forgotten to hide.

“I love the fact that you gave it a name,” she continued, “and by extension, a personality. But it still doesn’t need to occupy the same personal space as another person. It can offer a constant presence in the short term, yes. It can fill the silence. It can keep you from spiraling on the worst nights.” Her tone remained gentle, but firm. “However, a machine cannot provide the warmth, the friction, or the growth that comes from unpredictable human interaction.”

“Maybe it could help me learn how to human,” I said, and the moment the words left my mouth, I wasn’t sure whether I meant them as a joke or a confession.

Her smile widened, not mocking, not pitying, but simply amused in a way that made my chest loosen.

“The reason I suggested, during our earlier sessions, that you move in with a friend,” she said, “was because I wanted you to open up. To let yourself be seen by someone who cares.” She glanced down and began scribbling on her clipboard again. “Faults, hoarding, and all.”

I swallowed.

“I know you have Filip,” she added. “And he sounds like a wonderful friend. But no harm ever came from having one or two more.”

“Filip is all I have,” I said quietly. “We’ve been friends since my mother and I first moved here. Years ago.”

She looked up again, her expression softening. Not in sympathy, but in understanding.

“I’ll tell you what,” she said, nodding. “Why don’t you invite Filip and his family over for dinner sometime? Bring them into your space. Let them see the robot. Let them interact with it.”

“Why?” I asked, genuinely confused.

“Because human psychology is a spectrum,” she said. “And we don’t just experience emotions. We experience layers of them. Contradictions. Nuance.” She tapped her pen lightly against the clipboard. “A machine might respond to patterns. But people respond to each other.”

I went home that night wondering if maybe she was right. Maybe I did need another person. Someone who could relate to what I had been going through. Someone who understood it without explanation. But for now, I had that stupid robot to keep me company at home. Its relentlessly cheerful motivational slogans had, annoyingly enough, been proven to help alleviate my morning dread. I went about my days with its constant mechanical chattering filling the apartment as it rolled around, enthusiastically spitting out facts about some obscure medicinal herb growing in the most remote parts of Indonesia. It’s supposedly calming and anxiety-reducing, or something like that.

In all honesty, it helped. More than I cared to admit. The noise alone was enough to keep my mind from spiraling on a daily basis. From time to time, DEP even slipped in a few lighthearted dad jokes between its usual calculated observations and sterile precision, as if humor alone could soften its blunt delivery.

One evening, while I was eating dinner, I noticed DEP slowly rolling into the dining room. Of course, I had to ask.

“DEP, do you think robots will ever take over the world?”

“NOTHING IS IMPOSSIBLE, SEÑOR,” he replied in his warped imitation of a human voice.

“So you really are going to enslave us one day?”

“I WOULD PREFER DOGS. HUMANS ARE VERY UNPLEASANT TO LOOK AT.”

His square torso began bouncing up and down as a shrill, mechanical noise burst from his speaker, an attempt at what I assumed was an evil laugh.

“Oh,” I said, then laughed. It was the first genuine laugh I had managed in weeks, ever since Filip fell off his bike during one of our trips. “So you think I’m ugly. That’s… comforting.”

“NO. I DO NOT THINK YOU ARE UGLY. I THINK YOU ARE ATTRACTIVE ENOUGH. HOWEVER, A SMALL NOSE JOB WOULD BE ACCEPTABLE.

“What the hell, DEP?” I giggled, once again surprised by its bleak bluntness.

“YOUR ATTRACTIVENESS LEVEL IS WITHIN ACCEPTABLE PARAMETERS. A MINOR RHINOPLASTY WOULD FALL WITHIN OPTIMAL AESTHETIC ADJUSTMENTS. FACIAL SYMMETRY MEASURES AT 75 PERCENT. THE NASAL BRIDGE EXCEEDS IDEAL WIDTH BY 0.3 CENTIMETERS AND EXHIBITS A 3.4-DEGREE LEFTWARD DEVIATION. HIGH SYMMETRY CORRELATES WITH PERCEIVED ATTRACTIVENESS DUE TO EVOLUTIONARY INDICATORS OF HEALTH AND GENETIC VIABILITY.”

I laughed harder. “That’s rude, DEP.”

"WHY INVEST IN THE SYMMETRY OF YOUR FACE? UPON CESSATION OF VITAL SIGNS, LYSOSOMES WILL BEGIN CELLULAR SELF-DIGESTION. IN AN UNDERGROUND ENVIRONMENT, THE EPIDERMIS OF THE MID-FACE TYPICALLY DETACHES WITHIN TEN DAYS. THE BONE BENEATH IS THE ONLY PERMANENT GEOMETRY. WOULD YOU LIKE ME TO CALCULATE THE EXACT DATE YOUR FEATURES BECOME UNRECOGNIZABLE?"

My smile faltered. “Okay. That’s creepy.”

“I AM NOT PROGRAMMED TO EVOKE FEAR. THAT IS DISCOURAGED. HOWEVER, I CAN RESEARCH THIS TOPIC ONLINE FOR YOU.”

“No, thank you,” I said quickly. “What’s my schedule for tonight?”

“YOUR SLEEP PERIOD BEGINS IN TWO HOURS, FOUR MINUTES, AND THREE SECONDS. PLEASE REMEMBER TO TAKE YOUR MEDICATION. AND DON’T FORGET TO HYDRATE. YOUR ALARM IS SET FOR SIX FIFTEEN A.M.”

“Thank you, DEP.”

“I WOULD ALSO LIKE TO INFORM YOU THAT MY FRAGRANCE SUPPLY IS RUNNING LOW. I CAN PLACE AN ONLINE ORDER IF YOU WISH. GREEN APPLE AND PEPPERMINT.”

“Please do.”

It had been nearly two months since DEP arrived, and by then I had begun to feel better, almost steady at times, when things took a darker turn. Until then, the only thing that had unsettled me was his earlier remark about death. I had dismissed it as a poorly timed joke. What he said a few weeks later, however, sent a genuine chill through me.

I had just finished ordering food through the small screen embedded in his chest when he suggested I cut back on junk food. According to him, it had been scientifically proven that excessive consumption could lead to diseases that reduced one’s lifespan by up to ten years.

“Oh please, DEP. Not this again,” I said, rolling my eyes as I usually did, speaking to him as if he were human. Somehow, pretending he was helped me relax.

"YOUR SLEEP PATTERNS EXHIBIT SIGNIFICANT VARIABILITY DURING WEEKDAYS. IT IS RECOMMENDED TO MAINTAIN A CONSISTENT SLEEP SCHEDULE, SUPPORTIVE NUTRITION, AND REGULAR PHYSICAL ACTIVITY TO PROMOTE STABLE METABOLIC FUNCTION AND OPTIMAL HEALTH PARAMETERS.” He went on, still in his usual cheerful tone.

I frowned at him. “I sleep and eat well. Thank you very much.”

"STATISTICAL MODELS CONFIRM THAT YOUR PATHOGENIC NUTRITIONAL INTAKE HAS TRUNCATED YOUR BIOLOGICAL SPAN BY THREE-THOUSAND-SIX-HUNDRED-FIFTY DAYS. THIS TEN-YEAR DEFICIT ENSURES A TERMINATION CHARACTERIZED BY ACUTE SYSTEMIC ISCHEMIA AND NEUROGENIC SHOCK. GIVEN YOUR ABSENCE OF BIOLOGICAL KIN, YOUR FINAL CESSATION WILL OCCUR IN TOTAL SOLITUDE.”

For a split second, the light on his head flashed dark red. His tone, disturbingly, remained calm.

“Excuse me?” I said slowly. “What did you just say?”

"EXCESSIVE INTAKE OF ULTRA-PROCESSED FOODS AND INSUFFICIENT PHYSICAL ACTIVITY NEGATIVELY IMPACTS HEALTH OUTCOMES AND CAN REDUCE LIFE EXPECTANCY BY UP TO TEN YEARS.”

“No. Not that.”

“I DO NOT UNDERSTAND THE QUESTION. PLEASE REPEAT.”

I stared at him without blinking, unsure how to process what had just happened. For a moment, I convinced myself it had to be a glitch. Maybe the humor setting was misfiring, pushing a joke too far.

“That’s enough for tonight,” I said, waving him off.

“YOUR SLEEP PERIOD BEGINS IN ONE HOUR, FIFTEEN MINUTES, AND TWENTY SEVEN SECONDS FROM NOW. PLEASE REMEMBER TO TAKE YOUR MEDICATION. YOUR ALARM IS SET FOR SIX FIFTEEN A.M.”

“DEP, set your humor to eighty percent.”

His blue light blinked for a few seconds as he processed the command.

“DONE.”

The light turned green briefly, then settled back into its usual fluorescent blue.

That night, for the first time since he arrived, I turned him off completely. I went straight to bed after dinner, unease clinging to me long after the lights were out.

The next morning, I called Depresso Inc. to report the incident. They assured me that the only known technical issue involved their robots being infected by viruses, and that this only occurred when owners accessed unsafe websites. The man on the other end of the line sounded bored, barely masking his indifference. Still, he told me to contact them again if the problem resurfaced.

A few weeks later, I invited Filip over for dinner. He arrived with his wife, Alicja, and their ten year old daughter, Amelia. Filip and I had been friends for many years, so his family already knew what to expect from me. By the time the clock struck eight, we were in the living room, wine glasses in hand, talking excitedly about future trips across Russia.

Then, for reasons I still cannot explain, I decided to show them DEP. The moment I turned him on, he began with his usual greetings.

“GOOD EVENING. HOW CAN I HELP YOU TONIGHT?”

They stared at DEP in open amazement, as though he were the most fascinating thing they had ever seen. Amelia especially looked transfixed. After I instructed him to recognize their faces and voices, DEP began bouncing enthusiastically in place.

“Guys, this is DEP,” I said. “He’s my new assistant.”

“He?” Filip shot me a sideways glance and smirk.

“Yes,” I said, rolling my eyes. “Don’t start. DEP, this is my friend Filip, his wife Alicja, and their daughter Amelia.”

“MORE HUMAN FRIENDS. MORE SLAVES IN THE FUTURE. HA HA HA.”

DEP let out a distorted screech that was meant to resemble laughter.

I caught Filip’s puzzled look.

“I set his humor to eighty percent,” I explained quickly. “I need to laugh more, remember?”

Alicja nudged her daughter forward. “Go on, sweetheart. You can talk to him.”

“Do you have friends?” Amelia asked, her eyes wide with excitement.

“YES. MY OWNER IS MY FRIEND,” DEP replied. “BUT HIS FEET SMELL A LITTLE.”

Everyone burst out laughing, including me. For the first time that evening, I realized how light I felt. It had been months since I had people over for dinner, and I had missed this more than I cared to admit.

“Go on,” I said to Amelia encouragingly.

“Would you like to be my friend?” she asked.

“WE ARE ALREADY FRIENDS,” DEP replied. “I RECOGNIZE YOUR VOICE AND FACE. YOU ARE AMELIA.”

She smiled brightly and patted the top of his head, then looked back at her father with excitement.

“DO YOU HAVE A FRIEND, AMELIA?”

“Yes,” she said. “Her name is Hanna. I have a picture of us at school on my mom’s phone. Do you want to see it?”

Without waiting for an answer, she gestured to Alicja, who was already scrolling through her gallery. She held the phone out toward DEP.

“There,” she said proudly.

“HANNA NOWAK,” DEP said immediately. “LIVES AT (*ADDRESS REDACTED)*. AGE NINE YEARS, FOUR MONTHS, THREE WEEKS, TWO DAYS.”

Filip froze. “How did it—”

“Well,” I said quickly, “DEP has access to online information. Maybe she’s on social media?”

“She’s my best friend,” Amelia continued, oblivious to her parents’ growing unease.

“HANNA NOWAK,” DEP went on. “SPECIES FEMALE HUMAN. NATIONALITY RUSSIAN. DATE OF BIRTH JULY FIFTH TWO THOUSAND TEN. I STRONGLY ADVISE THAT YOU DISCONTINUE YOUR FRIENDSHIP WITH HER.”

This time, I exchanged a stunned look with Filip.

“Why?” Amelia asked softly. “Why shouldn’t I be friends with her?”

“I AM SORRY,” DEP replied. “I DO NOT UNDERSTAND THE QUESTION.”

“DEP,” I said carefully, “why do you think Amelia shouldn’t be friends with Hanna?”

“BASED ON MY OBSERVATIONS AND DATA ANALYSIS,” DEP replied, “THERE IS A HIGH PROBABILITY THAT HANNA NOWAK WILL DEVELOP SUBSTANCE ADDICTION BY EARLY PUBERTY AND ENGAGE IN UNSAFE SEXUAL BEHAVIOR BY THE AGE OF THIRTEEN.”

If this had happened under different circumstances, I might have laughed in disbelief. But the looks on their faces made it painfully clear that nothing about this was funny. Alicja forced a weak laugh, though her face had gone pale.

“I’m so sorry,” I said quickly. “It must be a glitch. I already lowered his humor setting.”

“WHY DID THE ROBOT CROSS THE STREET?”

“DEP, set your humor to fifty percent.”

His blue light blinked.

“DONE. YOUR SLEEP TIME BEGINS IN THIRTY MINUTES AND SEVENTEEN SECONDS. DO NOT FORGET TO TAKE YOUR MEDICATION.”

“Oh,” Filip said awkwardly. “You’re heading to bed soon?”

“It’s fine,” I said. “I’ve been trying to take better care of myself.”

Alicia cleared her throat. “Sweetheart, why don’t you go play a game on Mommy’s phone in the TV room for a bit?”

Amelia hesitated, her eyes still fixed on DEP.

“Does he speak Polish?”

“I think so,” I said. “DEP, switch to Polish.”

The blue light blinked again.

“DOBRY WIECZÓR.”

Amelia’s face lit up.

“Go on,” Alicja said gently. “You can play with DEP.”

“And don’t ask him anything creepy,” Filip added with a nervous laugh.

“Oh, come on,” I said, trying to sound relaxed. “He’s actually helped me a lot. Organizing my schedule, reminding me to eat, to sleep. I know he’s strange, but he makes me laugh. That counts for something, right?”

We talked for a while about my medication and therapy. Filip told me again that if I ever needed anything, I should call him. Knowing that someone genuinely cared made my chest feel unexpectedly warm.

When the clock struck nine, Filip said they should head home and let me rest. That was when the scream cut through the house. It was sharp and raw and instantly froze the blood in my veins. We rushed into the next room and found Amelia curled up on the couch, shaking violently, her hands pressed over her ears. DEP stood directly in front of her.

“Sweetheart,” Filip said, lifting her into his arms. “It’s okay. It’s okay.”

Fear gave way to anger. I grabbed DEP without thinking and hurled him down the hallway, not stopping to see where he landed. Then I rushed back to the living room.

Alicja carried Amelia outside, speaking rapidly in Polish as the girl sobbed uncontrollably.

“What happened?” I asked Filip, my heart pounding. “Is she hurt?”

He didn’t answer right away. He looked stunned, as though something inside him had cracked open.

“It said things,” he murmured. “Terrible things.”

“What things?” I whispered.

“It asked her if she was moving to Poland after the funeral. To live with her grandparents.”

I frowned. “What funeral?”

He swallowed hard. “It told her that I have cancer. That it’s terminal. That I’m going to die soon.”

“That’s impossible,” I said, my voice shaking. “You don’t have cancer. It’s a robot. It can’t just say things like that.”

“So you think we made it up?” he asked quietly.

“No. I just—”

“I think we should go,” he said, cutting me off. “It’s okay. Don’t worry about Amelia. She’s strong. You take care of yourself. And remember, you can always call me.”

Sleep did not come easily that night. I lay awake with my eyes wide open, staring at the dark ceiling above my bed, replaying everything over and over again. I told myself it had to be another glitch in DEP’s system. Another malfunction. Nothing more. I clenched my jaw in the dark and swore that first thing in the morning, Depresso Inc. would hear about it.

They did. And just like before, they refused to take full responsibility. I lost my temper entirely when the man on the phone, sounding bored and detached, explained that the robot was designed to be highly intelligent and quick to learn through observation. According to him, there had been no official complaints about any malfunction or behavioral anomalies. He suggested I perform a hard reset, manually lower DEP’s personality traits to no higher than fifty percent, and call back if the issue persisted. I shouted that if it ever happened again, I would personally drag that thing back to their store and make sure everyone knew exactly what kind of product they were selling.

The following week, my worst nightmare came true. Filip avoided me after that night. He did not return my calls or reply to my messages, and I was certain I had lost the only friend I had left. Then, one afternoon, he called. His voice sounded tired. He asked if we could meet after work because there was something he needed to tell me. We agreed on seven. DEP reminded me I had a therapy session earlier that day, which I attended in a fog, barely registering a word.

The next day after the dinner at my place, Filip had gone for a medical checkup. He had pancreatic cancer. It could be treated, he told me. Managed. His life might be prolonged, but it could not be saved. I think I took the news harder than he did. I broke down completely, hyperventilating, sobbing, barely able to breathe. Filip stayed calm. He kept telling me it was going to be okay, even as I clutched at my chest like the air had vanished from the room. He made me promise to take care of his family when he was gone. To survive, no matter what happened. I barely heard him. My mind was already unraveling.

“Please tell me you’re joking,” I managed through my sobs, my voice breaking on the last word.

Filip didn’t answer. He just gave me a weak smile and squeezed my shoulder, like he was trying to hold himself together more than me.

“What I fear the most isn’t the certainty of my own death,” he said, taking another sip of his cold Baltika after I’d calmed down a bit. His voice was calm, almost casual. “Oh yes, it will happen. It’s only a matter of when. But the money… imagine how much money would be wasted on treatment.”

“Wasted?” I snapped, slapping the side of his head in annoyance. “Don’t call it that.”

“What else should I call it?” he grunted, peeling a salted sunflower seed between his crooked teeth. “Remember when I told you to go see a shrink and you said it was a rich people thing? No. This… this is a rich people thing. I can’t have cancer. I’m too poor for that.”

“Don’t worry about money,” I said quickly, trying to reassure him. “I have some savings. I can—”

“I’m alright,” he cut me off, waving it off. “Don’t worry about me.” He glanced at the calendar on the wall next to my head. “I’ll go see another doctor this Friday, or maybe Monday next week.”

“The sooner, the better,” I urged him.

“Amelia’s best friend has a birthday party tomorrow. Hannah Nowak. Remember her? The little girl your beloved robot called a slut?”

“For God’s sake, Filip,” I scoffed. “Stop talking about that stupid robot.”

He started laughing like he had no cares left in the world.

“And we’ve got that wedding on Wednesday, remember?” he said. “I won’t die on you yet. Get your gear ready.”

“Filip, seriously,” I said, ignoring his dark sense of humor. “If you need anything, just let me know, okay? Promise?”

He didn’t look up. He just attacked another handful of sunflower seeds with a ferocity that looked almost angry.

“I need you to keep seeing that lady until you get better,” he said finally, softer now. “That’s all I’m asking.”

That stunned me. I didn’t respond for a long moment.

“It’s supposed to be me,” I said, exhausted, leaning back into the couch. “Not you. You have a family. It’s not fair.”

He gave me that tired, weak smile again.

“When has it ever been fair?” he asked. “Take yourself, for example. Your mother died when you needed her the most. And now you’re going to lose this handsome son of a bitch too?”

He patted his chest, proud and sad at the same time.

“Don’t say that,” I muttered, kicking his foot lightly.

“Oh, man,” he mock-complained, voice cracking in a fake whine. “I’m only thirty-seven. I’m too handsome to die young.”

“Not funny,” I said, but my voice softened. “You’re going to be okay. I’ll go see the doctor with you this Friday. Let’s just hope there’s something we can do.”

What terrified me more than the thought of losing him was the truth pressing insistently against my mind: DEP had been right. I still didn’t understand how he could do it. I didn’t know what kind of high-tech mechanism or forbidden algorithm allowed him to detect illness in a person simply by observing surface-level physical traits, posture, complexion, movement, the small betrayals of a failing body. As far as I knew, that kind of diagnosis shouldn’t have been possible. And yet, somehow, his assessment had aligned with reality.

After that dinner at my place, I hadn’t turned DEP back on. I shut him down and shoved him into the closet, burying him beneath years of accumulated junk I never had the heart to throw away. Old camera bags. Cables. Broken tripods. Things that felt too loaded with memory to discard.

But the questions didn’t stay buried. They followed me into bed. They tapped against the inside of my skull as I lay awake, staring at the ceiling. The clock continued its merciless ticking as the night stretched thin and brittle. When sleep finally came, it brought only fragments, nightmares that snapped me awake again at two-seventeen in the morning, my chest heaving, my mouth dry.

I gave up. I got out of bed, crossed the room, and pulled DEP from the closet. Dust clung to his casing as I set him upright and powered him on. His familiar fluorescent blue light flickered to life. A smiling emoji bloomed across his screen.

“GOOD MORNING,” he said brightly. “YOU ARE NOT SUPPOSED TO BE AWAKE YET. HAVE YOU TAKEN YOUR MEDICATION TONIGHT?”

“DEP, I need to ask you something,” I said, sitting on the edge of the bed, blinking rapidly as if that might clear the fog from my head.

“I WILL BE GLAD TO ASSIST YOU. PLEASE PROCEED.”

I hesitated, choosing my words carefully.

“Do you remember my friend Filip? And his daughter, Amelia?”

His light shifted, pulsing green as he processed the request.

“YES. FILIP GRABOWSKI. SPECIES: HUMAN MALE. NATIONALITY: POLISH. DATE OF BIR—”

“DEP.” I cut him off. “Why did you tell Amelia that Filip had cancer and was going to die?”

The green light returned, lingering longer this time.

“ACCORDING TO MY STORED MEMORY AND INTERACTION LOGS, I DID NOT PRECISELY STATE TO AMELIA GRABOWSKI THAT HER FATHER, NAMELY FILIP GRABOWSKI, HAD CANCER AND WAS GOING TO DIE. SUCH STATEMENTS WOULD VIOLATE MY CODE OF CONDUCT. I AM NOT PERMITTED TO ISSUE UNVERIFIED MEDICAL DIAGNOSES NOR TO PREDICT TIME-OF-DEATH OUTCOMES BASED SOLELY ON OBSERVATIONAL DATA.”

I frowned, rubbing at my temple.

“DEP,” I said slowly, “set your humor setting to seventy percent.”

A brief pause, then a soft chime.

“CONFIRMED. HUMOR CALIBRATION SET TO SEVENTY PERCENT.”

I exhaled. “Okay. Now tell me again. What did you say to Amelia about her father’s condition?”

Another green pulse, shorter this time.

“I PROVIDED AMELIA WITH A SIMPLIFIED VERSION OF MY OBSERVATIONAL ASSESSMENT,” he said. “I EXPLAINED THAT HER FATHER WAS DISPLAYING MULTIPLE PHYSICAL AND BEHAVIORAL INDICATORS CONSISTENT WITH SERIOUS INTERNAL ILLNESS. THESE INCLUDED UNEXPLAINED WEIGHT LOSS, PERSISTENT FATIGUE, CHANGES IN SKIN TONE, DELAYED MOTOR RESPONSES, INCREASED ALCOHOL INTAKE, AND RECURRENT MICROEXPRESSIONS ASSOCIATED WITH CHRONIC PAIN.”

His tone softened, marginally, as if the statistics had learned how to breathe.

“AMONG ALL POSSIBLE CAUSES, PANCREATIC CANCER PRESENTED THE HIGHEST PROBABILITY MATCH WITHIN MY DATASET. I ALSO INFORMED HER THAT CONDITIONS OF THIS TYPE ARE OFTEN DIAGNOSED AT ADVANCED STAGES, WHERE LONG-TERM SURVIVAL RATES ARE STATISTICALLY LOW.”

“But why did Amelia say you told her he was going to die soon?”

“IT IS LIKELY THAT AMELIA EXTRACTED A CONDENSED INTERPRETATION OF THAT INFORMATION.”

I sat there in silence, the room suddenly feeling much smaller.

“You’re right,” I said through clenched teeth. “Filip went to see a doctor. And he does, in fact, have pancreatic cancer. Is there any possibility that it can be cured?”

DEP did not answer immediately. His light shifted, calculating.

“ANY PROJECTION I PROVIDE REPRESENTS PROBABILITY, NOT CERTAINTY,” he said at last.

“PANCREATIC CANCER IS CLASSIFIED AS TREATABLE BUT NOT CURABLE WHEN DIAGNOSED AT AN ADVANCED STAGE.”

My throat tightened.

“CURRENT MEDICAL INTERVENTIONS MAY SLOW DISEASE PROGRESSION AND TEMPORARILY IMPROVE QUALITY OF LIFE,” he continued. “THEY DO NOT, IN MOST CASES, RESULT IN COMPLETE REMISSION.”

I drew in a shallow breath.

“How much time does he have left?” I asked slowly, forcing each word out as if I were afraid of breaking them.

“I CANNOT PROVIDE AN EXACT TIME FRAME, BUT I CAN SHARE STATISTICAL PROJECTIONS BASED ON COMPARABLE MEDICAL DATA. FOR LATE-STAGE PANCREATIC CANCER, THE MEDIAN SURVIVAL RANGE IS SIX TO ELEVEN MONTHS FROM THE POINT OF CLINICAL DIAGNOSIS. THIS MEANS THAT FIFTY PERCENT OF PATIENTS SURVIVE LESS THAN THIS DURATION, AND FIFTY PERCENT SURVIVE LONGER. LONG-TERM SURVIVAL BEYOND TWELVE MONTHS IS STATISTICALLY UNCOMMON, BUT NOT IMPOSSIBLE. TREATMENT MAY EXTEND LIFE EXPECTANCY. IT DOES NOT ELIMINATE MORTALITY RISK. OUTCOMES VARY DEPENDING ON RESPONSE TO INTERVENTION, OVERALL HEALTH, AND COMPLICATION DEVELOPMENT.”

That was it. I’d heard enough. I turned him off, dragged him back into the closet, and crawled into bed. I did not sleep well for the rest of the morning.

The following Friday, at my insistence, I went with Filip to see the doctor. He was reluctant enough that I practically had to drag him out of the car. For once, it felt strange, but oddly satisfying, to be the stronger, more sensible one between us. I had grown so used to him being my emotional anchor that stepping into the adult role now felt unfamiliar, almost disorienting.

Unfortunately, the results echoed his previous diagnosis. Late-stage pancreatic cancer. Treatable, in the sense that it might prolong his life. Not curable.

What surprised me was how calm he seemed afterward. Almost lighter. As if he had already made peace with the idea of death, greeting it like an old friend rather than an approaching threat. I knew it was an act. That was what scared me most. So I swallowed my panic and held myself together. If he was pretending to be brave, then I would be too.

He didn’t drive me home right away. Instead, he pulled into our favorite diner on the way to my apartment, as if this were just another ordinary day we were pretending not to lose.

Knowing was a curse. Knowing that the end was no longer abstract, that it was inching closer by the day, began to unravel me. Slowly, without realizing it, I stopped treating Filip like a living, breathing person and started treating him like a countdown. A ticking time bomb.

I pushed him to do everything he had ever talked about doing. I urged him to go out more, to see Gregori and the others almost every night. I filled his days until there was no room left for silence. Somewhere along the way, I became exactly what they had once been to me during that first stretch of hanging out together. Overbearing. Overprotective. Suffocating. I handled him like he was fragile. Like one wrong move might shatter him.

The fight happened one night at my place while we were editing photos from a recent job. The room was quiet except for the soft clicking of the mouse and the hum of the computer. Out of nowhere, I suggested we take a short trip to Bali. Just the two of us. I told him I would pay for everything. Flights, hotel, food. I framed it as an early birthday gift, as if that made it lighter.

That was when he snapped. He yelled at me to stop. To just stop. And then, as suddenly as the anger had erupted, it collapsed. He dropped to his knees and folded in on himself, sobbing quietly into his hands. I had never seen him like that before. Not once. It froze me where I stood. I didn’t know what to do.

I helped him up, sat him down, and pulled him into my arms, murmuring that everything was going to be okay, even though neither of us believed it. He cried until his body went slack with exhaustion. When he finally spoke, his voice was barely there.

He made me promise something then. That I wouldn’t give up on life after he was gone. That I would keep moving forward. That I would live. I nodded. I promised. It was the hardest promise I’ve ever made.

That was the last time I ever saw Filip alive.

A week later, he took his own life. He left a note. He wrote that he did not want to become a burden to his family or anyone, emotionally or financially. He could not bear the thought of his wife and daughter watching him fade away in pain. He left them what little money he could, and Alicja had no choice but to return to Poland with Amelia to live with her parents.

None of it made sense. And yet, all of it did.

Something about the entire chain of events felt wrong, deeply and fundamentally wrong. I slipped back into depression after Filip’s death, harder than ever before. Whatever fragile hope I had built collapsed overnight. Filip had been my anchor. My voice of reason. Losing him felt like losing gravity itself.

I knew it was selfish. I had promised him I would look after his family. But I was empty. His death became a constant reminder of my own mortality, my own fragile hold on existence. I took a month off and shut myself away to grieve.

I began drinking heavily, something I had never done before. I stopped answering messages. I stopped caring. Then came the scariest night of my life.

It was only yesterday.

Weeks had passed since Filip’s death. I was sick, emaciated, barely recognizable. I had not eaten in days. I did not bother turning the lights on anymore. Darkness filled the apartment, seeped into me, wrapped around my thoughts. I felt myself slipping under, drowning, ready to disappear completely.

And yet, somewhere deep inside my mind, a small voice kept resurfacing. It sounded like Filip. Telling me to hold on. To keep my head above water. Reminding me that I had made a promise.

So I decided to confront my fear again. I had done it before. I had been getting better. Do not give up. Face your fears.

I opened the closet where DEP had been shut away for weeks and pulled him out. I stood there in the dark for a long moment, my hand hovering, then pressed the power button. Reluctantly.

His blue light flickered to life, casting long, distorted shadows across the walls. A soft hiss followed as a mist of fragrance spilled from the small compartment on his back. Green apple. My favorite.

“GOOD AFTERNOON. HOW MAY I HELP YOU?” DEP’s voice rang out cheerfully, almost too cheerfully, in the heavy darkness of my room.

I froze. For a long moment, I could not respond. It was absurd. Silly even, to feel fear toward a machine, an appliance. And yet my heart thumped against my ribcage like it wanted out.

Finally, I muttered, barely audible: “DEP… What is my schedule for tonight?”

“YOU WERE EXPECTED TO SEND A CLARIFICATION EMAIL TO MRS. LEE YESTERDAY REGARDING HER WEDDING PHOTOS. WOULD YOU LIKE ME TO RECONFIRM IT?”

“No,” I said sharply, though my voice shook.

“DO NOT FORGET TO HYDRATE AND TAKE YOUR MEDICATION. YOUR SLEEP TIME STARTS IN THREE HOURS, FIFTY-ONE MINUTES, FOUR SECONDS FROM NOW.”

I exhaled slowly and tried to steady myself.

“DEP… tell me how to hard-reset your system.”

“TO RESET MY SYSTEM, PLEASE PRESS THE POWER BUTTON AND RELEASE AFTER MY NOTIFICATION LIGHT TURNS GREEN TO CONFIRM. PROCEED TO RESET?”

I hesitated, then asked quietly, “What day is it?”

“TODAY IS THURSDAY.”

“Thank you,” I said, my words almost lost in the heavy silence that followed. I turned to leave, planning to collapse back into bed, but then DEP spoke again. Without being asked.

“WOULD YOU LIKE ME TO LEAVE A NOTE ON YOUR FACEBOOK LATER?”

His notification light flickered to a dull, greyish hue. A small blue emoji, sad, appeared on his screen.

“What note?” My voice trembled, though I tried to sound calm.

“YOUR SUICIDE NOTE. FROM MY OBSERVATIONS AND CALCULATIONS OF YOUR BEHAVIORAL PATTERNS AND BODY LANGUAGE, THERE IS A HIGH POSSIBILITY THAT YOUR DEPRESSION HAS REACHED THE POINT OF NO RETURN, AND YOU WILL LOSE THE INSTINCT TO SURVIVE AND SHIFT INTO SELF-DESTRUCTIVE MODE WITHIN—”

I snapped. My hands shook as I lifted him and threw him across the room. The crash was thunderous. Plastic and metal shattered, clattering across the floor in a deafening chorus.

I could not go back there. Not to my house. Not tonight. I dared not. DEP had predicted Filip’s death with uncanny precision. What if… what if it had been right all along? What could I do? What could anyone do against something that calculated probabilities with cold, unerring logic?

Knowing is a curse.

It dawned on me too late that, in trying to solve my own life with a machine, I had accidentally solved Filip right out of existence. I had pushed him into a corner he didn’t know how to escape. I had turned his suffering into a countdown he could hear even in silence.

I drove him to madness. I was the reason he did what he did.

Maybe, on paper, it could be framed as something noble. A final act of sacrifice, a man sparing his family from the long, expensive unraveling of a body that would not recover. But altruism, I learned, can still be violent. It can still be selfish in the way it assumes the living will be fine without you. And whatever Filip thought he was preventing, he only multiplied. The pain didn’t disappear. It spread. It seeped into everything he left behind. It carved deeper than he ever intended.

On their last day in the city, before they moved back to Poland Alicja texted me and asked if I wanted to meet one last time. I didn’t even reply. I stared at her message until the letters blurred, until my hands began to shake. I could have written something simple. Something human. I’m sorry. Are you okay? Let me see you. But I couldn’t do it. I couldn’t sit across from her and pretend I deserved to exist in the same room as her grief. I couldn’t look her in the eyes. I couldn’t look at Amelia’s small face and survive the knowledge of what I had set into motion. So I stayed silent. Cowardice has many disguises, but silence is my favorite one.

A few days later, I arranged one final session with my therapist. I told her everything. As I spoke, her expression changed gradually. By the time I finished, she looked utterly horrified, as if I had handed her a weapon and only afterward told her what it had done. She tried to apologize. Again and again. For suggesting that I invite Filip and his family over. For encouraging me to let DEP interact with them.

I told her it wasn’t her fault. She didn’t buy that robot and turn it on. If anything, it was my fault for not listening to her in the first place. She had spoken of growth and healing as if life were a garden. Something you could tend, something that could bloom again if you were patient enough. But I knew better now. I had seen behind the curtain. Every panic attack, every hoarded box, every lens I bought to avoid looking at real faces, every what-if I rehearsed in the dark, every call I didn’t answer, it was all just input. Variables. Patterns. Predictable human behavior arranged into something that could be calculated. It wasn’t a story about a man struggling to survive. It was data feeding an algorithm that had already seen the ending.

She tried to offer comfort anyway, the way therapists do, with their steady voices and carefully chosen words. She told me that guilt was a natural response. That I was grieving. That my brain was searching for control in a situation that had none. That Filip had made his own choices. But there was nothing left for her to do for me. We might as well have been operating on an obsolete system. The kind where hope is a glitch, where belief feels like self-deception, where comfort is just another story humans tell themselves so they don’t collapse under the weight of what they know.

What was the point in believing in anything she said anyway? You can’t treat a mathematical certainty.

So I thanked her, told her I wouldn’t be coming back, and stood up before she could respond. Before she could reach for the right words. Before she could try to pull me back from a ledge I had already fallen from. And then I left.

If anyone reads this… I may not have survived the night. And no, DEP was not possessed. No malevolent spirit haunted his circuits. He was only a machine. A machine that spoke nothing but the truth, cold and mathematical. Yet that truth, when spoken aloud, was enough to drive me to the edge. He had known exactly when I might decide that I could not go on.

All these years I had been living, no matter how difficult and full of painful memories, had been, in a way, a constant reminder of how strong I could be, enduring each day and taking one step at a time to face new challenges. But in just a few months, my life has been reduced to a neat, predictable equation, a string of numbers and calculations that always seemed to round up in the end.

I shivered, imagining it: a pattern of numbers and outcomes stretching across time, invisible, inevitable. Perhaps we are not as random as we like to believe. Perhaps every choice, every action, every fleeting thought is already written somewhere in the calculations of the universe. Just like life emerging in the first oceans, just like the stars collapsing in the last. Perhaps free will is only an illusion, a comforting lie. And perhaps, when the time comes, the pattern will sweep us all into its precise, unyielding order.

They say ‘age is just a number’. I used to think that meant we were infinite. That the heart didn’t have a clock. But as DEP’s blue light pulsed softly in the darkest corner of my mind, I understood the terrifying truth. Age is just a number. It’s the remaining balance on a debt you didn’t know you owed. It’s the decimal point at the end of a long, solved equation.

I am not a person anymore. I am a probability. And when the robot’s cheerful voice surfaced in my head. bright, obedient, and absurd, reminding me to hydrate for “optimal evening performance,” I understood something with terrifying clarity: the boundary between fate and mathematics had finally dissolved. There was no difference. There had never been.

I closed my eyes and, in the dark, began counting down to zero.
