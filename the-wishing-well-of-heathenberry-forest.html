THE WISHING WELL OF HEATHENBERRY FOREST

Deep within the forbidden Heathenberry Forest lies an ancient well surrounded by strange rules and darker legends. One night, a young boy decides to find out for himself what lurks below.

✦ ✦ ✦

I threw a rock down the well. It clattered against the walls, bouncing from one side to the other. I waited, straining my ears. There was no indication that it had reached the bottom.

“Hello?” I whispered, my voice low and unsteady, as I peered into the darkness, my eyes unable to penetrate its inky depth. The sound of my voice reverberated against the sodden, ancient walls, breaking into a discordant choir of echoes that disturbed the stillness of the enclosed space. Something felt wrong. I knew I wasn’t supposed to be there.

It had taken most of the evening for me to gather the courage to cross the barren land alone and enter Heathenberry Forest. Jamie Coldon’s words had echoed in my mind the entire way to the clearing.

“If something answers you, just make sure you don’t tell it your name," he had warned me the day before, as we walked along the edge of the forest, adjacent to an old, dilapidated train tunnel to the north.

“Why not?” I had asked. “And what will answer me?”

He had stared up at the reddening summer sky, his pale blue eyes sparkling with amusement.

“You ask too many questions, kid. Stupid questions. Just don’t fucking do it, all right? Not many people know where to find that stinkin’ old well. They go there to be told whether they’re going to be successful or not in their lives. You toss a dime in for each question. Make it quick. Never ask questions whose answers you don’t want to know. Most importantly… never tell it your name. Or it’ll steal your soul.”

I had gasped and cringed. I’d never heard anyone my age use language that harsh before. But Jamie had always liked acting cool, older than he really was, especially when he wanted attention. Besides, he was older than me.

“You’re kidding, right, Jamie?”

“The only reason I’m talking to you at all is because I feel sorry for you. Your father was a good man. He helped my family a lot. I’m not doing this for you, cretin. Kathy and I are leaving for the city next week. Don’t tell anyone I told you that. We’ve got everything figured out. Her friend has a place for us somewhere. We’re getting married as soon as we’re gone. We’re not coming back. Her father would skin me alive if he ever saw me again after I ran off with his only daughter.”

His pale, round face twisted into a mix of determination, hope, and anguish as he went on.

“If you’re smart, you’ll get out of this place before you lose your mind, too. These people are hopeless. Or go to that well. Whatever. If that makes you happy, I don’t care. Just wait until midnight. Make sure nobody follows you or knows where you’re going. You have to do it alone, or it won’t answer you.”

He pointed west, toward a dark bluish hill looming over the distant plain.

“See that hill? There’s a narrow trail at the top. Follow it. It’ll lead you due south into the forest. When the bushes grow so thick you can barely push through them, you’ll reach a small clearing in the middle of the woods. From there, you can weave easily through the trees. That’s where the well is. Don’t follow the trail west beyond the clearing. There are marshes. People have drowned trying to cross that godforsaken plain.”

And he had been right. The air was chilly when I emerged from the treeline. I found myself standing in a small, roughly circular clearing, devoid of anything taller than knee-high dandelions. There, in the middle of the miniature savanna, almost hidden in the darkness from probing eyes, stood a small, dilapidated stone mound. Its structure was blackened and sunken.

“Hello?” I called out again, louder but still hesitant, my features tightening . This time, a dreadful silence filled the well. There were no echoes, as if it had swallowed my voice whole. Confused, I bent down to pick up another rock from the ground.

“Hello?”

A voice suddenly rose from below. I gasped in horror and recoiled, only to trip over my own feet. I fell backward and landed hard on my buttocks. As I sat there on the cold, hard ground, waiting, convinced the devil himself was about to rise from his subterranean tomb to claim me, something flashed across my mind. A cautionary tale Jamie had told me earlier that day, before he left for the apple farm where he had been working for years.

“You dare summon it, you have to finish your business there. Never forget to say thank you and goodbye when you’re done. Otherwise it will think you are not finished and follow you around, watching you from the shadows wherever you go.”

I struggled to my feet and stood there, staring intently at the derelict stone mound, frowning at the ancient symbols etched into its rough surface. My chest heaved rapidly with fear and uncertainty. That voice had stirred something inside me I never knew existed. A primal fear of the unknown. Of what waited beyond the darkness.

“Hh.. hello?” I chirped.

“Hellooo…” it drawled, its voice laced with a contempt that suggested concealed danger. It sounded warm, yet distant. There was something unnervingly familiar about it. It almost sounded like my own voice, only distorted, much deeper, and feline.

“Do not be afraid. Come closer,” it hissed, almost inaudibly.

“What… what are you?” I stammered.

“Well, I am a wood fairy,” it replied, letting out a gleeful chuckle.

An image of a tiny, winged human with pointed ears and delicate features instantly drifted through my mind, one that did not match the malicious voice rising from below.

“Really?”

“Yessss,” it hissed loudly, as if forcing air through gritted teeth. “You see those symbols? They are called Ogham inscriptions. An ancient spell to keep me here. I cannot hurt or touch you, even if I wanted to.”

“I… I need your help. Will you help me?”

I was afraid of engaging further with this disembodied being, but I knew it was the only way. Nothing else would work.

“What is it?”

“I miss my father.” I began to sob. My shoulders shook violently as I pressed my face against the cold, rugged stone, feeling hopeless and defeated. Soon, indignation and outrage swelled inside me once more. I barely noticed my surroundings. The forest had fallen silent. The psithurism of leaves in the wind ceased all at once, as if the land itself were holding its breath in anticipation.

“What is it?” it asked again, colder now, more demanding.

I lifted my face from the stone and stared into the darkness below, confused and disoriented. As I narrowed my eyes, trying to catch even the slightest movement, I felt the forest closing in. It seemed to dissolve into shadowy walls pressing from every direction, driving me toward the inky void.

“Hello?” I leaned over the mound and arched my body to peer down.

“Tell me, what is it?”

“What d…”

“What would you like me to do?”

I opened my mouth, but the words would not come. I retreated a step, thinking it might not be a good idea after all. Maybe I should just go home and never return to this blasted heath.

“You can tell me. What is it you want? I can help you,” it said coyly.

“Can you bring him back to me?” The words escaped my lips before I could stop myself.

A few seconds of silence followed, long enough to almost make me turn on my heels and run. It felt wrong talking to something I could not see, something hiding in the dark, the very embodiment of darkness. I wondered what was real and what was not. . Even then, I knew I was trespassing beyond the border of reality.

“Of course,” it said.

“Rreally?” I asked.

“Would you believe me if I told you your father was down here with me, looking up at you? And that all you had to do was jump down to be with him.”

“Uhm, I do not think that is a good idea.” I took a step back from the well and rubbed my face. For one thing, I knew exactly where my father was. Six feet under the ground at Roccocherry Cemetery, a few miles west of the outskirts of town, where we had buried him weeks earlier. Not at the bottom of an old, dilapidated well.

The concept of death was something I was still struggling to grasp. For all I knew, he could be somewhere else entirely, waiting. I was only a kid, and a faint flicker of skepticism passed through my thoughts as I considered its words. Would I even survive the fall if I couldn't see the bottom? Was it telling the truth?

A cold, high-pitched laugh rose from below, devoid of emotion. It sounded hollow and insincere.

“You are one smart kid.”

“So can you really…” I hesitated.

“Yes. I will bring your father back to you. Would it make you happy to see him again? Surely it would.”

“What is the catch then?” I asked cautiously. “What do you want from me?”

“Very bold of you to assume I want anything at all from you.”

“Then what…”

“What is your name, dear?”

I opened my mouth to answer, but Jamie’s words from the day before flashed across my mind.

“I am not allowed to tell you my name.”

“Why, now that is a lie. Who told you that?”

“My friend.”

“Well, what do we know about this funny friend of yours? Is he not as huge and slow as a grazing bovine on a summer day?”

“Everyone knows the rules. We cannot tell you our names.”

“Well, everyone does not want to bring their father back from the dead, do they? But you do. You have been missing him a lot, have you not? And it is killing you. This is a very big favor you are asking of me. Besides, I only want to be your friend. Are friends not supposed to know one another’s names? Or we can stay on a last name basis, if that makes you more comfortable.”

“Is there any other way th…”

“Oh yes. There is.”

“What is it? Please tell me.”

“I need human sacrifice. Blood.”

“Wwhat… did you just say? Blood? Human blood?”

It burst into a mirthless laugh, unpleasant and cold.

“Would it not be much easier if you simply told me your name, my dear boy?”

For a moment, I considered going along with it. All I had to do was tell it my name, and my father would return to me. It was not complicated. I opened my mouth, but whatever instinct had kept me from speaking for the last twenty minutes suddenly surged back to the surface.

“Yes, my friend? Your name. Tell me your name,” it said soothingly. “And you and your dear father will be reunited. That is all it takes.”

It took every ounce of willpower to break my silence. Between heaving breaths and gritted teeth, I finally relented. It was the right thing to do.

That night, I stumbled upon the truth about Heathenberry Forest, as it spoke to me of things far beyond my comprehension.

***

Katherine Deanshaw’s eyes were puffy and red when I saw her in the village the following day. She looked troubled, unhinged. For the briefest moment, she glanced in my direction as if she wanted to ask me something. Under different circumstances, if only things had been different, I would have been more than willing to listen. She had always been a nice girl.

She waved at me and smiled uncertainly, then walked away with John Clearwater, the son of the richest man in the village, the boy her father had always wanted her to be involved with. It pained me that I could not help her. Truly, it did.

But I had to disengage from any responsibility that was not my own. I was waiting for things to fall into place. For my father’s return. It was the only thing that mattered to me.

I was only fourteen, after all.
